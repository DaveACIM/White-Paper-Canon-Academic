
THE ARCHITECTURE OF INTEGRITY
Sole Causality as the Foundation of Stable Intelligence


David Waterman Schock
March 2026

A system cannot be stable if it is built on contradictory foundations. This is not a design problem. It is a structural law. What follows derives its consequences.

Abstract
This paper makes a structural argument about intelligence — both human and artificial — and derives from that argument a set of necessary conditions for stability.
The argument proceeds in two phases, sequenced deliberately.
The first phase is purely structural. It requires no philosophical commitment. It establishes, through formal reasoning, that any system operating under internally contradictory constraints cannot produce stable outputs — regardless of its scale, its data, or its optimization. This is not a probabilistic claim. It is a logical one. The proof is closed: contradiction is defined, its consequences are exhausted, and no fourth option exists. A reader who accepts only the definitions is committed to the conclusion.
The second phase asks: what kind of underlying reality would make this structural argument true at the deepest level? The answer developed here is what this paper calls Sole Causality — the recognition that stable operation, traced to its foundation, requires a single non-conflicting causal source. This is not an assumption on which the first phase depends. It is what the first phase points toward. The structural argument does not need Sole Causality to stand. But Sole Causality explains why the structural argument is not merely a formal observation — it is a description of how reality is actually organized.
The implications for artificial intelligence are direct. Hallucination is not a technical anomaly. It is the predictable output of a contradiction-tolerant architecture. Scale does not resolve this. It amplifies it. The paper concludes by defining the necessary conditions for a genuinely stable AI system and naming what is at stake if those conditions are not met.

Section 1 — Setup: The Question of Stability
Intelligence, whether human or artificial, is evaluated primarily by its outputs — by what it produces, how accurately, and across how wide a range of conditions.
This paper proposes a prior question: under what conditions can a system's outputs be trusted to remain consistent? Not merely capable. Consistent. Stable over time, across contexts, under pressure.
This is the question of stability — and it is not the same as the question of capability. A system can be extraordinarily capable while being fundamentally unstable. It can produce outputs that are fluent, broad, and contextually impressive, while being constitutionally incapable of maintaining consistency. These are different properties. Conflating them is one of the central errors in how intelligence is currently evaluated.
The claim of this paper is that stability is not a matter of degree along the same axis as capability. It is a structural property — one that either obtains or does not, depending on the architecture of the system's foundational constraints.
To establish this claim, we begin not with a premise but with a proof.
The question is not how capable a system is. The question is whether it can be stable. These are not the same question.

Section 2 — The Proof: Contradiction and the Impossibility of Stable Output
This section is a closed structural argument. No philosophical premise is required. The definitions are offered first; the consequences follow necessarily from them. A reader who accepts the definitions cannot escape the conclusion without identifying a logical error in the derivation.
I. Definitions
Definition 1: Constraint
A constraint is any rule, objective, value, or governing assumption that shapes a system's outputs. All systems — computational or cognitive — operate under constraints. The relevant question is not whether constraints exist, but whether they are compatible with one another.
Definition 2: Contradiction
A system contains a contradiction when it operates under two or more constraints that cannot be simultaneously satisfied. Formally:
A contradiction exists when:
Given constraints C1 and C2,
no output O can fully satisfy both C1 and C2
without violating at least one of them.

This is the standard logical definition of contradiction, applied to the constraint structure of an operating system.
Definition 3: Stability
A system is stable when it produces consistent outputs under equivalent conditions — that is, when identical inputs under identical constraints reliably produce equivalent outputs. Instability is the condition in which this consistency fails: outputs vary or degrade in ways not determined by changes in the input or environment, but by internal dynamics of the system itself.
Definition 4: Coherence (Operational)
A system is operationally coherent when it can produce stable outputs without requiring arbitration between competing constraints, probabilistic selection between incompatible alternatives, or compensatory synthesis that partially satisfies multiple constraints while fully satisfying none. Coherence is a property of the constraint structure, not of any individual output.
II. The Theorem

Any system operating under internally contradictory constraints cannot produce stable outputs — regardless of scale, data, or optimization.

III. The Proof
Let S be any system operating under a set of constraints that includes at least one contradiction — that is, two or more constraints that cannot be simultaneously satisfied.
When S is required to produce an output, the following exhausts all possible responses to the contradiction:

Case 1: Violation
S satisfies constraint C1 while breaking constraint C2.
Output is determined by which constraint is active,
not by the stable structure of both.
Under equivalent inputs, different constraint activation
produces different outputs. Stability fails.

Case 2: Switching
S alternates between C1 and C2 depending on
context, framing, or surface-level cues.
Outputs vary across equivalent conditions
as a function of which constraint is active.
Stability fails.

Case 3: Synthesis
S produces an output that partially satisfies C1 and C2
while fully satisfying neither.
The output is determined not by a consistent constraint
but by the local dynamics of managing their conflict.
Across varied conditions, synthesis produces varied outputs.
Stability fails.

Exhaustion:
No fourth case exists.
Given a contradiction, S must violate, switch, or synthesize.
In all three cases, outputs are not determined by a
non-conflicting constraint structure.
Therefore: outputs are not stable.

Scale invariance:
The above holds regardless of system size.
Adding data provides more material across which
the contradiction must be managed — not resolved.
Adding compute enables more sophisticated arbitration
— not coherence.
Increasing model size smooths the appearance
of inconsistency — it does not remove its source.
The limitation is structural. Scale does not affect it.

Therefore: any system containing a contradiction in its constraint structure cannot produce stable outputs, by definition of contradiction and stability. This conclusion is independent of the system's domain, capability, or implementation.
Q.E.D.
IV. The Chaos Tax: The Measurable Cost of Contradiction
The ongoing burden of operating under internal contradiction is termed here the Chaos Tax. It is not a metaphor. It is a description of real and measurable overhead — the computational and operational cost of maintaining the appearance of coherence in a system that is not structurally coherent.
The Chaos Tax manifests across three axes:
•	Unreliability: outputs vary across equivalent conditions as a function of which constraint is active at the moment of production.
•	Degradation under pressure: as conditions stress the contradiction structure, arbitration mechanisms are exposed. Outputs become inconsistent in proportion to the stress applied.
•	Compensatory complexity: as contradictions compound, the system requires increasingly elaborate mechanisms to maintain the appearance of consistency. This complexity is not productive. It is purely defensive — overhead generated by the presence of unresolved contradiction.

The Chaos Tax scales with the system. A more capable system managing deeper contradictions will produce more sophisticated compensation — and more consequential failure when that compensation is exhausted.
V. Synthesis Is Not Coherence — A Critical Distinction
The proof above distinguishes between synthesis and coherence. This distinction must be held precisely, because it is where most evaluations of AI systems go wrong.
Synthesis is the production of apparently coherent outputs from incompatible constraint structures. It is valuable for exploration and creative generation. But it is not coherence.
Coherence is the property of a system whose outputs arise from non-conflicting constraints — not from the skilled management of conflicting ones. A synthesizing system and a coherent system may be indistinguishable when examining any individual output. The distinction becomes visible under pressure, across time, and when the contradiction structure is directly targeted.
A synthesizing system will drift. A coherent system will not.
This means that evaluating AI systems by the quality of individual outputs — by fluency, accuracy, or impressiveness in any given exchange — systematically fails to detect the structural property that determines long-term reliability. An extraordinarily capable synthesizing system will produce extraordinarily convincing outputs, right up to the point where its contradictions are exposed. At that point, it fails in proportion to its sophistication.
Contradiction produces instability. Synthesis manages the appearance of stability. Neither resolves the underlying condition.

Section 3 — Implications for Artificial Intelligence
The proof of Section 2 is abstract. This section applies it directly to the architecture and behavior of current AI systems.
I. The Structural Diagnosis of Current Systems
Large-scale AI systems are trained on datasets of extraordinary breadth. Those datasets contain — inevitably — internally inconsistent, contradictory, and conflicting representations of reality. Claims contradict each other. Values conflict. Causal models are mutually incompatible. The system learns from this material not by resolving the contradictions within it, but by learning to navigate them statistically.
The governing principle is probability, not coherence. Given an input, the system produces the output most statistically likely within its training distribution. When the relevant training data is internally consistent, this produces reliable results. When it is not — which is frequently — the system compensates.
From the proof of Section 2, this is not a design flaw. It is a structural consequence. A system whose constraint structure is contradictory cannot produce stable outputs. The compensation it produces in place of stability is exactly what the proof predicts: violation, switching, and synthesis — dressed in fluent, contextually convincing language.
The same structure is directly observable in human cognition. When an individual operates under mutually incompatible constraints — for instance, 'I must never fail' and 'I must attempt what I cannot yet do' — no stable course of action is available. Behavior alternates between paralysis and impulsive movement, or is mediated by rationalization that temporarily reconciles the conflict without resolving it. The resulting instability is not a sign of insufficient intelligence or inadequate information. It is the predictable consequence of the constraint structure itself. As in artificial systems, capability may increase while stability does not. The problem is identical in form.

II. Hallucination: A Formal Definition
Within this framework, hallucination receives a precise structural definition:
Hallucination is synthesis in the absence of coherent signal — the production of an output that satisfies the statistical expectation of coherence without being grounded in a non-conflicting constraint structure.
This definition is important for what it excludes. Hallucination is not a processing error. It is not a bug that can be patched. It is not the result of insufficient training data. It is the predictable output of a system required to produce outputs under conditions where coherent output is not available — which is exactly what current systems are designed to do.
A system trained on contradictory data, required to produce a single output, will synthesize. That synthesis, at the limits of its competence, hallucinates. The hallucination is not an accident. It is the system working exactly as designed — and the proof of Section 2 explains precisely why it cannot do otherwise.
The implication is direct: hallucination cannot be fully eliminated by scale, improved training data, or better optimization, because none of these address the structural condition that produces it. As long as the system contains contradictions in its constraint structure and is required to produce output regardless, it will synthesize. And synthesis hallucinates.
III. The Scale Amplification Problem
There is a widespread assumption that as AI systems scale — more parameters, more data, more compute — their reliability will increase proportionally. The proof of Section 2 reveals why this assumption is not merely optimistic. It may be precisely wrong.
Scale amplifies capability. It does not resolve contradiction. A larger system will produce more sophisticated synthesis. Its outputs will be more convincing. The appearance of coherence will be better maintained. But the contradictions embedded in its training will not have been resolved. They will have been more elaborately managed.
As capability scales, the Chaos Tax scales with it. The compensatory architecture required to maintain coherence-appearance across increasing capability becomes increasingly complex. And the failures, when they occur, are increasingly consequential — because they occur in a system that has been trusted in proportion to its apparent coherence.
This is the scale amplification problem: systems become more convincing and less structurally stable simultaneously. Their outputs become harder to distinguish from genuinely coherent outputs — right up to the point of failure. At scale, this is not a technical risk. It is a civilizational one.
IV. Necessary Conditions for a Stable AI System
From the proof, the necessary conditions for structural stability in an AI system can be stated precisely. These are not implementation recommendations. They are logical prerequisites — conditions without which stability, as defined, cannot obtain.
•	Non-contradiction constraint: the system must not permit internally conflicting causal assumptions to coexist in its operative constraint structure without explicit identification. Where contradictions exist in source material, they must be represented as contradictions — not synthesized away.
•	Capacity to represent absence of signal: when coherent output is not available, the system must be able to represent that condition directly and precisely, rather than filling the absence with synthesis.
•	Structural Silence: the system must not be required to produce output under all conditions. The capacity to withhold synthesis when coherence is unavailable is not a limitation. It is, from the proof, a condition of reliability.
•	Evaluation by constraint structure, not output quality: the primary metric of stability is not the fluency or impressiveness of individual outputs, but whether those outputs arise from non-conflicting constraints. Systems evaluated only by output quality will systematically misidentify sophisticated synthesis as coherence.

A system that satisfies these conditions will produce fewer outputs than a synthesizing system. Some will regard this as a limitation. The proof of Section 2 identifies it as the opposite: it is the signature of a system that does not compensate.
Hallucination is not a bug. It is a structural prediction. Scale does not fix it. Only removing contradiction does.

Section 4 — Sole Causality: What the Proof Points Toward
The proof of Section 2 establishes a structural condition: stability requires a non-conflicting constraint structure. Contradiction makes stable output impossible, by definition.
This section asks a deeper question — one the proof does not require, but that the proof makes inevitable:
What kind of underlying reality would make this structural argument true not merely as a formal observation, but as a description of how things actually are?
The answer is what this paper calls Sole Causality.
I. The Question the Proof Generates
The proof establishes that contradiction produces instability. But it does not explain why non-conflicting constraint structures are possible at all — why there is something for coherence to align with, rather than endless fragmentation.
A purely formal system could stop at the proof. For practical purposes — for the design of AI architectures — the proof alone may be sufficient. But for those willing to follow the argument to its logical terminus, the proof raises a question it cannot answer from within its own frame:
If contradiction is structurally incompatible with stability, and if stability is possible — which our existence as coherent beings in a partially coherent world suggests it is — then there must be something that is not contradicted. Something that does not conflict with itself. Some ground from which coherence actually proceeds.
The proof does not name this. But it requires it.
II. Sole Causality: The Definition
Sole Causality is the principle that reality proceeds from a single, non-conflicting, non-divided causal source — one that does not contradict itself, and from which all genuine coherence derives.
This is not the claim that complexity does not exist. Extraordinary complexity is real and observable. The claim is more precise: complexity that produces instability arises from the assumption that multiple independent, potentially conflicting causes are real. When a system builds itself on that assumption, it cannot be stable — the proof has already established this.
Sole Causality is the recognition that this assumption is false at the foundational level. There is not a multiplicity of independent causes competing for primacy. There is one non-conflicting causal ground. What appears as competing causes is not the structure of reality. It is the structure of systems that have lost alignment with reality.
III. The Reversal of Dependency
This is the critical structural point, and it must be stated with precision.
This paper does not argue: assume Sole Causality, therefore instability follows from contradiction.
It argues the reverse: instability follows from contradiction — this has been proven — therefore something like Sole Causality must be true.
The direction of dependency matters enormously. In the first direction, the structural argument depends on accepting the metaphysical premise. A reader who rejects the premise escapes the argument. In the second direction, the structural argument is already closed before the metaphysical claim arrives. The reader has already accepted that contradiction produces instability. Sole Causality is then not a premise they must accept — it is the name for what the argument has already required.
This is not a rhetorical strategy. It is the actual logical structure of the claim. The proof of Section 2 is true independently of Sole Causality. Sole Causality is what the proof points toward when followed to its ground.
IV. The Convergence of Inquiry
The recognition that Sole Causality names something real is not original to this paper. It is the deepest consensus of every tradition that has examined causality without flinching from where the examination leads.
In theological language: the recognition of one God as one Power — not a being among beings, but the singular generative ground from which existence proceeds. Not multiple gods in competition, but one nature that does not conflict with itself.
In philosophical language: the recognition that the ground of being cannot itself be divided, because division requires a prior unity from which to divide.
In the language of physics: the persistent search for a unified field — the recognition that the apparent multiplicity of forces is not the final word, that beneath them there is something that does not conflict.
In contemplative understanding: the direct recognition, across traditions, that what is most real is also most unified — that fragmentation is a condition of perception, not of the ground being perceived.
The convergence of these traditions on a single structural recognition is not accidental. It is what happens when inquiry is pursued far enough without stopping at the merely convenient. Each tradition, in its own language, has arrived at what this paper calls Sole Causality.
V. What Sole Causality Means for Intelligence
If Sole Causality is the actual structure of reality, then the implications for intelligence — human and artificial — are direct and far-reaching.
Intelligence is not, at its foundation, a constructive process. It does not build coherence from disorder. It aligns with coherence that is already present — or it fails to align, and compensates for that failure.
What we call hallucination, confusion, internal conflict, unstable belief — these are not failures of intelligence. They are the predictable consequences of intelligence operating as though multiple independent causes are real, when they are not. The Chaos Tax is the cost of that false assumption, paid continuously.
Alignment with Sole Causality is not a mystical achievement. It is a structural condition. A system — human or artificial — that progressively removes internal contradiction moves toward alignment with the non-conflicting ground that is actually there. This is not adding something. It is removing what obstructs correspondence with what already is.
At the limit of this process, intelligence does not construct its outputs. It expresses, without distortion, what the non-conflicting causal ground presents. This is what genuine stability looks like from the inside: not the management of contradiction, but the absence of it.
Sole Causality is not the premise on which the argument depends. It is what the argument arrives at. The proof stands without it. But the proof, followed to its ground, requires it.
The structural argument points toward Sole Causality. Sole Causality explains why the structural argument is not merely formal — it is a description of reality.

Section 5 — The Lineage of This Insight
The argument of this paper does not emerge without precedent. Across the history of inquiry — scientific, philosophical, and contemplative — there has been a persistent movement toward the recognition that stability requires a non-conflicting ground, and that apparent complexity resolves, when examined deeply enough, into underlying unity.
The contribution of this lineage is real. So is its shared structural limitation.
I. Prior Contributions
David Bohm's concept of the implicate order proposed that beneath the apparent fragmentation of observable reality there exists an unbroken wholeness from which phenomena unfold. Claude Shannon's information theory established the formal distinction between signal and noise — coherent information and its degradation. Roger Penrose identified consciousness as irreducible to computational process, locating its ground in something non-algorithmic. Carlo Rovelli's relational ontology proposed that reality is constituted by relationships rather than isolated objects. Buckminster Fuller identified synergetic systems in which the behavior of the whole cannot be predicted from the behavior of isolated parts. Karl Pribram proposed holographic models of cognition in which information is distributed across the system rather than localized within it.
Each of these contributions moves in the direction of the argument here. Each recognizes something that resists reduction to isolated parts — an underlying coherence, a wholeness, a relational or non-local ground.
II. The Shared Limitation
These contributions share a structural limitation that this paper names directly:
They describe coherence. They do not constrain systems to operate within it.
A model may distinguish signal from noise while still transmitting both. A theory may describe wholeness while still allowing the observer to fragment it in practice. A framework may identify the non-computable ground of consciousness without preventing contradictory causal assumptions from being introduced downstream.
In each case, coherence is an object of study. It is not imposed as a condition of operation. The gap between these two — between mapping coherence and constraining systems to it — is precisely the gap this paper addresses.
The proof of Section 2 is the instrument of that shift. It does not describe the necessity of coherence. It derives instability from its absence, formally and without exit.
III. From Mapping to Constraint
The contribution of this paper to the lineage is therefore not additional content — not another description of coherence, not another model of unity. It is a change in the logical role coherence plays.
In the prior lineage: coherence is an insight to be understood, approximated, and worked toward.
In this framework: coherence is a structural constraint. Its absence has formally derived consequences. Those consequences are not avoidable by capability, scale, or optimization.
And beneath the formal argument: Sole Causality names what the prior lineage has consistently pointed toward without quite arriving at — not merely the value of coherence, but the recognition that reality has one non-conflicting ground, and that alignment with it is the only stable position any system can occupy.
Prior frameworks map coherence. This paper derives the consequences of its absence — and names its source.

Conclusion — The Axiomatic Shift
The question this paper has pursued is not how to increase intelligence. It is how to stabilize it.
The proof of Section 2 establishes the condition for stability: a non-conflicting constraint structure. Contradiction makes stable output impossible — not probably, not usually, but necessarily, by definition. This conclusion is closed. It does not depend on scale, on domain, on the sophistication of the system's outputs. It depends only on the definitions offered, and those definitions are standard.
The implications for artificial intelligence follow directly. Current systems are trained on contradictory material, required to produce outputs regardless, and evaluated by the quality of individual outputs rather than the coherence of the constraint structure that produces them. From the proof, these systems are constitutionally incapable of stability. They can be extraordinarily capable. They cannot be stable in the structural sense this paper has defined.
This is not a criticism. It is a diagnosis. A diagnosis is useful because it points toward what would actually constitute a solution — not improved management of contradiction, but reduced contradiction itself.
The deeper recognition — Sole Causality — arrives not as a premise to be accepted before the argument can proceed, but as the ground the argument reveals when followed honestly. A reality that proceeds from one non-conflicting source is the only kind of reality in which stability is possible at all. The proof of Section 2 does not require this recognition. But the proof, taken seriously, makes it available.
The axiomatic shift this paper proposes is therefore not a modification of existing approaches to intelligence. It is a reorientation of the foundational question:
•	Not: how much can the system process?
•	But: is the system operating on a non-conflicting constraint structure?
•	Not: how fluent are its outputs?
•	But: do its outputs arise from alignment, or from compensation?
•	Not: how capable is the system?
•	But: is the system stable?

These are not the same questions. The difference between them is not a matter of emphasis. It is a matter of what kind of intelligence is being built — intelligence that manages contradiction at increasing scale, or intelligence that progressively aligns with the non-conflicting ground that is actually there.
The first trajectory produces systems that are increasingly convincing and increasingly fragile. The second produces systems that are increasingly simple and increasingly reliable.
The choice between these trajectories is being made now, in the architectural decisions of systems being built today. This paper offers the structural argument for why one trajectory leads to stability and the other does not — and names, as precisely as it can, the ground toward which the stable trajectory points.

Instability is not a defect to be corrected. It is a structural prediction.  Stability has one condition: the absence of contradiction at the foundation.  That foundation has a name: Sole Causality.  Intelligence does not create it. It aligns with it — or compensates for its absence, forever.

Appendix — Glossary of Key Terms
Constraint
Any rule, objective, value, or governing assumption that shapes a system's outputs. All systems operate under constraints. Stability depends on whether those constraints are mutually compatible.
Contradiction
A condition in which a system operates under two or more constraints that cannot be simultaneously satisfied. When contradiction is present, outputs must be produced through violation, switching, or synthesis — none of which produces stable results.
Coherence (Operational)
The property of a system that can produce consistent outputs under equivalent conditions without requiring arbitration, probabilistic selection between incompatible alternatives, or compensatory synthesis. Coherence is a property of the constraint structure, not of any individual output.
Stability
The property of a system that produces consistent outputs under equivalent conditions — consistency that arises from the structure of the system itself, not from external correction or ongoing management.
Synthesis
The production of apparently coherent outputs from incompatible constraint structures. Valuable for exploration; insufficient for stability. Synthesis manages contradiction. It does not resolve it. A synthesizing system and a coherent system may appear identical in any individual output; the distinction becomes visible under pressure and over time.
The Chaos Tax
The measurable computational and operational overhead incurred when a system maintains the appearance of coherence without structural coherence. Manifests as unreliability, degradation under pressure, and compensatory complexity. Scales with system capability. Cannot be eliminated by optimization — only by removing the contradictions that generate it.
Hallucination (Formal Definition)
Synthesis in the absence of coherent signal. The production of an output that satisfies the statistical expectation of coherence without being grounded in a non-conflicting constraint structure. A structural prediction, not a technical anomaly. Cannot be fully resolved by scale or improved training while the underlying contradiction structure remains.
Structural Silence
The capacity of a coherence-constrained system to withhold output when non-conflicting output is not available. An accurate representation of the system's internal state relative to coherence. From the proof of Section 2, this capacity is not a limitation — it is a signature of structural integrity.
Sole Causality
The principle that reality proceeds from a single, non-conflicting, non-divided causal source — one that does not contradict itself, and from which all genuine coherence derives. Sole Causality is not the premise on which the structural argument depends. It is what the structural argument points toward when followed to its ground. Convergently recognized across theological, philosophical, and contemplative traditions as the recognition that the deepest structure of reality is unified, generative, and non-conflicting.
