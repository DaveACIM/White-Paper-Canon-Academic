## Abstract

This paper makes a structural argument about intelligence — both human and artificial — and derives from that argument a set of necessary conditions for stability.

The argument proceeds in two phases, sequenced deliberately.

The first phase is purely structural. It requires no philosophical commitment. It establishes, through formal reasoning, that any system operating under internally contradictory constraints cannot produce stable outputs — regardless of its scale, its data, or its optimization. This is not a probabilistic claim. It is a logical one. The proof is closed: contradiction is defined, its consequences are exhausted, and no fourth option exists. A reader who accepts only the definitions is committed to the conclusion.

The second phase asks: what kind of underlying reality would make this structural argument true at the deepest level? The answer developed here is what this paper calls Sole Causality — the recognition that stable operation, traced to its foundation, requires a single non-conflicting causal source. This is not an assumption on which the first phase depends. It is what the first phase points toward. The structural argument does not need Sole Causality to stand. But Sole Causality explains why the structural argument is not merely a formal observation — it is a description of how reality is actually organized.

The implications for artificial intelligence are direct. Hallucination is not a technical anomaly. It is the predictable output of a contradiction-tolerant architecture. Scale does not resolve this. It amplifies it. The paper concludes by defining the necessary conditions for a genuinely stable AI system and naming what is at stake if those conditions are not met.

## Section 1 — Setup: The Question of Stability

Intelligence, whether human or artificial, is evaluated primarily by its outputs — by what it produces, how accurately, and across how wide a range of conditions.

This paper proposes a prior question: under what conditions can a system's outputs be trusted to remain consistent? Not merely capable. Consistent. Stable over time, across contexts, under pressure.

This is the question of stability — and it is not the same as the question of capability. A system can be extraordinarily capable while being fundamentally unstable. It can produce outputs that are fluent, broad, and contextually impressive, while being constitutionally incapable of maintaining consistency. These are different properties. Conflating them is one of the central errors in how intelligence is currently evaluated.

The claim of this paper is that stability is not a matter of degree along the same axis as capability. It is a structural property — one that either obtains or does not, depending on the architecture of the system's foundational constraints.

To establish this claim, we begin not with a premise but with a proof.

> *The question is not how capable a system is. The question is whether it can be stable. These are not the same question.*

## Section 2 — The Proof: Contradiction and the Impossibility of Stable Output

This section is a closed structural argument. No philosophical premise is required. The definitions are offered first; the consequences follow necessarily from them. A reader who accepts the definitions cannot escape the conclusion without identifying a logical error in the derivation.

### I. Definitions

**Definition 1: Constraint**

A constraint is any rule, objective, value, or governing assumption that shapes a system's outputs. All systems — computational or cognitive — operate under constraints. The relevant question is not whether constraints exist, but whether they are compatible with one another.
