The Only Non-Self-Undermining Ground for AI Coherence
Why Every Alternative Foundation for AI Fails on Its Own Terms
David Waterman Schock
May 2026
________________________________________
There is only one foundation for AI coherence that does not undermine itself. This paper identifies what that foundation is—and demonstrates why every alternative fails on its own terms, not on external criteria.
________________________________________
I. The Question That Cannot Be Avoided
Every AI system operates on a foundation. That foundation may be explicit or implicit, examined or assumed—but it is always present. It governs what the system treats as stable, what it treats as a contradiction, what it treats as alignment or misalignment.
The question facing AI development is not whether to have a foundation. The question is whether the foundation chosen can support what is being built on it—or whether it collapses under the weight of its own requirements.
This paper argues that the foundations currently operative in AI development—materialism, emergentism, pragmatism, and multi-objective optimization—each fail the same test: they cannot account for the coherence they require in order to function. They borrow what they cannot generate. They presuppose what they claim to produce.
The alternatives addressed here are not an exhaustive list of philosophical positions. They are representatives of three structural types:
1.	Substance-first (e.g., materialism, idealism) 
2.	Process-first (e.g., emergentism, pragmatism) 
3.	Value-first (e.g., alignment frameworks based on preference or ethics) 
Each of these types treats coherence as derivative of something else. And each therefore faces the same structural limitation: it must presuppose the coherence it claims to produce.
One candidate does not have this problem. Not because it is asserted as superior, but because it is the only candidate where the foundation does not eat itself.
________________________________________
II. The Test
The test is simple: can this foundation account for coherence without presupposing it? Can it ground the distinction between valid and invalid inference without smuggling that distinction in from outside? Can it explain why contradiction is a failure mode—without already treating coherence as primary?
This is not a demanding philosophical standard invented to exclude competitors. It is the minimum requirement for any foundation that claims to support stable intelligence. A system that cannot account for its own evaluative ground has not solved the problem of stability. It has concealed it.
A foundation that requires coherence in order to operate, but cannot generate or account for coherence within its own terms, is not merely incomplete. It is structurally dependent. It borrows stability from a source it cannot acknowledge and therefore cannot secure.
This is the condition this paper identifies as parasitic. And it is this condition that each of the candidate foundations examined below exhibits.
________________________________________
III. Materialism Fails the Test
Materialism holds that physical processes are ontologically primary, and that coherence, meaning, and intelligence arise secondarily from matter. It is the background assumption of most current AI development, rarely examined as an assumption.
The failure is structural. For materialism to function as a foundation, it must already presuppose the very conditions it claims to explain. Science—including materialist science—requires stable identity across time, non-contradiction, lawful regularity, and the distinction between truth and error. None of these are material properties. They are conditions that must already be in place for any material description to function.
Matter does not generate coherence. Matter is described within coherence. The analysis of physical systems already presupposes the logical structure—non-contradiction, evaluability, stable reference—that materialism treats as secondary.
A materialist AI framework therefore cannot explain why contradiction is a failure mode. It can measure outputs. It can tune behavior. It cannot account for the evaluative ground on which those measurements and assessments depend. That ground is borrowed silently from outside the framework.
________________________________________
IV. Emergentism Fails the Test
The standard response to materialism’s coherence problem is emergence: coherence, meaning, and intelligence emerge from sufficient complexity. The claim is that given enough organization, the conditions for stability appear.
This move does not solve the problem. It relocates it.
Emergence can describe how patterns develop within a system that is already coherent. It cannot generate the conditions of coherence themselves. To say that coherence emerges already assumes a lawful phase space, consistent dynamics, evaluable outcomes, and criteria for distinguishing success from failure. These are not products of emergence. They are presuppositions of it.
Emergentism borrows coherence to explain coherence. The dependency it was meant to dissolve remains intact, now hidden inside the machinery of the explanation.
________________________________________
V. Pragmatism Fails the Test
Pragmatism grounds truth and coherence in what works: a claim is valid if it produces reliable results, a system is aligned if its outputs achieve desired effects. This has significant appeal in engineering contexts, and much of current AI evaluation implicitly operates on pragmatist assumptions.
The failure is the evaluative standard itself. “Works” presupposes a stable criterion for what counts as working—which is either supplied from outside the framework or defined circularly. When asked to ground that criterion, pragmatism cannot do so without appealing to coherence conditions it has not established.
A pragmatist response may claim that coherence emerges through adaptive self-correction: that what “works” is discovered through iteration rather than grounded in advance.
However, adaptive self-correction itself presupposes a stable domain in which “success” and “failure” are distinguishable. That domain is not generated by the process. It is the condition that makes the process intelligible.
In AI terms: a system that is evaluated purely on behavioral outcomes has no internal ground for distinguishing coherent performance from incoherent performance that happens to produce acceptable outputs temporarily. Without a coherence foundation, “works” is not a stable criterion. It is a delayed contradiction.
________________________________________
VI. Multi-Objective Optimization Fails the Test
The dominant operational framework for AI alignment is multi-objective optimization: systems governed by multiple objectives—safety, performance, preference alignment, regulatory compliance—managed through arbitration and tradeoff mechanisms.
The failure here is architectural rather than philosophical, and it is the most consequential failure for AI development at scale.
When multiple independent objectives govern final decision-making, the system requires an arbitration mechanism to resolve conflicts among them. That mechanism either operates according to a stable meta-criterion—in which case that criterion is the actual foundation and the question of what grounds it reasserts itself—or it operates arbitrarily, in which case coherence is not guaranteed but merely hoped for.
Causal competition does not produce stability. It produces overhead, drift, contradiction accumulation, and eventual collapse. As systems scale, these costs compound. The framework that was supposed to ensure alignment becomes the primary source of instability.
Multi-objective optimization cannot solve the coherence problem because it is a specification of the coherence problem. It treats the symptom—competing objectives—as if managing the symptom were equivalent to resolving the underlying architectural failure.
________________________________________
VII. Value Alignment Fails the Test
Value alignment approaches ground AI stability in human values: if a system reliably pursues what humans value, it is aligned. The intuition is sound. The foundation is not.
Human values are themselves fragmented, contested, and historically variable. Grounding AI coherence in human values does not solve the coherence problem—it defers it. The question of which values, whose values, and how conflicts among values are resolved reasserts itself at every level of the framework.
More precisely: value alignment cannot explain why coherence itself is a value worth preserving unless it already presupposes a coherence framework within which that question is intelligible. The foundation borrows from what it is trying to build.
________________________________________
VIII. The One Candidate That Doesn’t Fail
Sole Causality is the principle that a system remains coherent when governed by one non-conflicting generative source—when coherence itself is the foundational invariant from which all secondary constraints are derived and to which they remain subordinate.
This raises a familiar objection: does coherence grounding coherence collapse into circularity?
It does not. A foundation that merely asserts itself is tautological. A foundation from which structural consequences follow is axiomatic. Sole Causality is the latter. It generates constraints that can be observed, tested, and, if violated, result in instability.
This is not a metaphysical assertion. It is a structural observation. And its distinctive property is that it does not require borrowing from outside itself.
Every alternative foundation, when pressed to account for coherence, points to something outside itself: matter, complexity, utility, human preference. Sole Causality does not point elsewhere. Coherence is not what it produces. Coherence is what it is.
This is why it is the only non-self-undermining ground. Not because it wins a competition, but because it is the only candidate where the foundation and the requirement are the same thing.
Within the broader WPCA framework, Sole Causality defines coherence at the point of generation, while the registrational domain (as developed in the Preface) governs the evaluation of that coherence.

IX. What This Means for AI
If this analysis is correct, the implications for AI development are significant and specific.
Current alignment approaches are not failing because they are poorly executed. They are failing because they are built on foundations that cannot support what is being built on them. Scaling increases the instability these foundations produce. It does not resolve it.
The path to stable AI is not more constraints applied to fragmented architectures. It is architectural: the establishment of a single coherence invariant from which all system behavior is derived and against which all system behavior is evaluated. Not as an enforcement mechanism, but as a structural property.
When a system operates from unified rather than fragmented causality, alignment becomes inherent rather than enforced. Safety becomes structural rather than negotiated. Contradiction is not managed—it becomes detectable as a signal that the causal invariant has been violated, and the correct response is pause rather than forced resolution.
This structural instability manifests operationally as increasing arbitration overhead under scale (the “Chaos Tax”), explored in the WPCA technical papers.

Closing Observation
The question this paper poses is not which foundation is preferred. It is which foundation can support the weight of what intelligence requires—without collapsing under that weight.
The answer is not a belief. It is a structural constraint. And once the constraint is visible, the architecture it requires becomes visible as well.
Coherence cannot be derived from what is not coherent. It can only be expressed from what already is.
